Part 3 — Scale · Chapter 14

Caching and the prompt cache

ttlMs, cacheScope, deterministic list ordering, and why reordering your tools costs your users money.

Status draftProtocol 2026-07-28Code git checkout ch14-caching

Chapter 8 measured what your tool surface costs once. This chapter is about what it costs every turn, and the answer depends on something most servers treat as an implementation detail: the order your tools come back in.

Two caches, and only one of them is yours#

The revision added explicit caching to the protocol.

Specification 2026-07-28

Results from tools/list, prompts/list, resources/list, resources/read and resources/templates/list carry required ttlMs and cacheScope fields via a new CacheableResult interface. ttlMs is a freshness hint in milliseconds; cacheScope ("public" or "private") controls whether shared intermediaries may cache the response. Both complement the existing listChanged notifications.

That is the protocol cache: it saves round trips. Useful, and not the expensive one.

The expensive one is the model's prompt cache, and you never address it directly. Your tool definitions sit near the front of the context window, ahead of the conversation. A provider caches that prefix and charges a fraction to re-read it. The cache is a prefix match: everything up to the first difference is a hit, and everything from there on is paid for in full.

Which means a single reordered tool invalidates the cache from that point onward — even though not one byte of your tool surface actually changed.

The load-bearing file nobody reviews#

FastMCP returns tools in registration order. ledger registers by importing modules for their side effects, so the order comes from the order of the import lines in server.py.

Before this chapter, that order was: the scan tools, then the catalogue tools, then the write tool — because scans is imported before tools, because the lines happen to be alphabetical by module name. Move an import, or let a linter sort them differently, and every user's prompt cache misses on their next turn. No reviewer flags it. Nothing in the diff says "performance".

Four methods, and the deploy that reorders an import stops mattering. The test is the useful part:

assert names == sorted(names), (
    "tools/list must be deterministic — registration order depends on the "
    "import order in ledger.server, which nobody reviews as load-bearing"
)

And the one that matters more, because it catches the failure a single-instance test cannot see: two instances must produce the same order, or half your requests invalidate the cache the other half just warmed.

What else moves in your prefix#

Ordering is the one people miss. Three others are worth a moment.

Anything with a timestamp in it. A tool description mentioning "as of today", a resource whose content includes a generation time, an instructions block with a version string that changes on every build. Each of those is a guaranteed miss, once per deploy or once per day, for every user.

Per-request tailoring. Chapter 19 will filter the tool surface by caller identity, which is the right thing to do and which fragments the cache by definition. That trade is worth making — a smaller surface beats a cached large one — but make it knowingly rather than discovering it.

Large results, inserted mid-conversation. Chapter 8 flagged this and it belongs here properly. A 15,118-token result does not just cost 15,118 tokens once. It lands in the middle of the conversation, and everything after it is new prefix. The paging in chapter 10 bought more than it appeared to.

ttlMs, cacheScope, and what your library will not send#

ledger cannot emit the protocol cache fields. FastMCP 4.0.3 does not put them on list results, and chapter 2 showed the captured responses proving it.

This is worth sitting with rather than skipping, because it is the ordinary condition of building on a moving protocol: a required field that your library does not yet produce. Three options, in the order most people should consider them.

Do nothing, knowingly. Clients written against the revision must tolerate their absence — the same backward-compatibility rules that cover a missing resultType. You lose the caching hint, not correctness. Write it down in a drift log, with the date.

Patch it in your app. Possible, and it means owning a shim through every library upgrade to add a field nobody currently reads.

Fix it upstream. The honest answer for a gap this small and this mechanical.

What you should not do is claim conformance you do not have. A client that requires ttlMs will not be satisfied by a server that does not send it, and chapter 24 is where "which protocol version do you actually speak" has to be answered out loud.

Invalidation, now that subscriptions changed#

Specification 2026-07-28

resources/subscribe and the HTTP GET endpoint are replaced by subscriptions/listen: one long-lived POST-response stream that a client opts into by notification type — toolsListChanged, promptsListChanged, resourcesListChanged, resourceSubscriptions — with each notification tagged by io.modelcontextprotocol/subscriptionId.

The design point underneath: ttlMs handles the common case of "this is probably still fine", and notifications handle "this definitely changed". Together they should make polling rare, which is the outcome the revision was after.

ledger's catalogue does not change during a conversation, so it needs neither. Most servers are like this and announce changes they do not have. If your tool list is fixed at startup, say so with a long TTL when you can send one, and do not open a stream to tell nobody about nothing.

Caching inside your server#

Everything above is about the client's cache. Your own is a smaller matter and mostly ordinary engineering, with one MCP-specific wrinkle.

Cache keys must include the caller's identity the moment your server is multi-tenant. Chapter 17 makes ledger multi-tenant, and a cache keyed on title_id alone at that point serves one studio's data to another. It is the most boring possible way to cause a serious incident, and it is common enough that cacheScope: "private" exists in the protocol to stop intermediaries doing the same thing.

Checkpoint#

The cache you cannot see is the one that costs money. Sort your lists, keep timestamps out of your prefix, page your results, and when you fragment the cache deliberately — for per-caller surfaces, in Part 4 — know that is what you are doing.
